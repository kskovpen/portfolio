Yesterday at CERN I stopped in front of a small plaque in a quiet corridor. It says that in these offices all the basic technologies of the World Wide Web were developed. A good place to start this blog, which is, after all, just a page on the web.

<!--more-->

![The plaque at CERN: "Where the web was born"](web-plaque.jpg)

## A problem physicists had

In March 1989 Tim Berners-Lee, a British computer scientist at CERN, wrote a short proposal called "Information Management". The problem was very practical. Thousands of physicists from institutes all over the world worked on the same experiments, on different computers, and information kept getting lost. His boss wrote on the cover: "vague but exciting".

The idea was simple: put documents on computers connected to the internet and link them to each other, so that anyone can jump from one to the next with a click. Together with the Belgian engineer Robert Cailliau, he made it real. By the end of 1990 the first web server, the first browser and the first web page were running on a computer at CERN.

## A gift to everyone

On 30 April 1993 CERN made the web software free for anyone to use, without asking and without paying. That decision is a big part of why the web grew into what it is today. The very first website is still online at [info.cern.ch](https://info.cern.ch).

## The same need today

Experiments at CERN are still huge worldwide teams. In [CMS](/science/cms/), the experiment I work on, several thousand people from more than 50 countries share data, code and results every day. The web was made for exactly that. Today CERN also relies on a worldwide computing grid that links computer centres on every continent to store and analyse the data from the Large Hadron Collider.

People often ask what fundamental research is good for. The web is one of the best answers. It was not planned as a product: it came from physicists who simply needed to share what they knew.
