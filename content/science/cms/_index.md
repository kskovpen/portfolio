CMS is one of the two giant general-purpose detectors at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC). It is 21 metres long, 15 metres high and weighs 14,000 tonnes, more than the Eiffel Tower. Built in layers around the point where protons collide, it can record up to 40 million collisions every second. In 2012 CMS and ATLAS together discovered the Higgs boson.

Each layer has its own job. Close to the collision, a **silicon tracker** follows the paths of charged particles. **Calorimeters** around it stop most particles and measure their energy. A huge **superconducting magnet** bends the paths of charged particles, which reveals their momentum, and the outer **muon chambers**, placed between the layers of the magnet's iron, catch muons: the only particles that get through everything else.

## What I do here

- **Top quark and Higgs boson.** I study the heaviest known particle, the [top quark]({{< relref "/science/cms/top-quark" >}}): I was one of the main people behind the [first observation of four top quarks produced at once]({{< relref "/science/cms/top-quark/four-tops" >}}), took part in the first observation of the Higgs boson produced together with top quarks, and searched for top quark decays that the theory almost forbids.
- **Heavy neutral leptons.** I search for hypothetical heavy cousins of the neutrino, which could explain why neutrinos have a mass at all.
- **Making the detector work.** I have led the teams that teach CMS to recognise particles coming from bottom quarks (2015–2019), that process the data and produce the computer simulations (2021–2023), and that measure how well the tracker reconstructs particle paths (2023–2025).
- **Building the next CMS.** For the High-Luminosity LHC, which will deliver ten times more collisions from the end of this decade, we build and test silicon modules for the new tracker and new muon detectors in Ghent.
